THE QUESTION

Who came first — and what can history actually prove?

Thiruparankundram is not one monument with one founding date. It is a rocky landscape containing ancient ascetic shelters, Hindu shrines, a major Murugan pilgrimage tradition, a dargah and a long record of changing use. The evidence comes from different sources — inscriptions, literature, architecture, archival descriptions and court records. They do not all answer the same question.

“The age of a tradition is not necessarily the age of the building that carries it today.”

THE EVIDENCE, IN TIME

A layered chronology

Dates below refer to different kinds of evidence. A literary reference, a dated inscription and a building's construction date are not interchangeable.

c. 2nd–1stBCE*
01 · EARLY HISTORIC PERIOD

Stone beds in the rock shelters

The hill contains natural caves, rock-cut beds and Tamil-Brahmi inscriptions associated with ascetic use. The Archaeological Survey of India's epigraphic study describes the cave inscriptions as the earliest records then available for the hill and notes Buddhist or Jain ascetic use in the early centuries CE. A later Jain epigraphic catalogue dates some beds to the 2nd–1st centuries BCE.

What it establishes: ascetic and religious activity on the hill in the early historic period. The precise dating of individual inscriptions should be checked record by record.

Physical evidence · dating varies
Early Tamilliterature
02 · LITERARY TRADITION

Parankunram in Murugan poetry

The Paripadal, a Tamil anthology grouped with the Sangam classics, contains poems describing Parankunram as a pilgrimage centre associated with Murugan or Subrahmanya. The ASI's study identifies several relevant verses and describes the hill's literary reputation.

The poems are evidence of a deep Murugan association. Their precise composition history and dating are scholarly questions. The ASI study itself notes that the relevant Paripadal verses could postdate the 773–774 inscriptions; they should not be used as a construction date for the surviving temple or as a simple proof of which tradition came first.

Literary evidence · dating debated
773–774CE
03 · PANDYA PERIOD

A rock-cut temple takes shape

Two inscriptions studied in Epigraphia Indica record the excavation of a rock-cut cave temple and the consecration of Sambhu (Shiva) in the sixth regnal year of the Pandya ruler Maranjadaiyan, equated by the epigraphist to 773–774 CE.

The complex described in the study includes shrines for Shiva, Durga and Vishnu, a lower shrine for Jyeshtha, and a Subrahmanya sculpture. This is a dated milestone in the temple architecture — not the date of every part of the present-day complex.

Two dated inscriptions · ASI publication
Medievalperiod
04 · ISLAMIC HERITAGE

The dargah and the Sikandar traditions

The hilltop shrine is known as the Sulthan Sikkandar Badusha Auliya Dargah. Its history is described through more than one tradition. A 2025 Madras High Court judgment discusses accounts that distinguish a 12th-century Syed Sikandar Badhusha from the 14th-century Alauddin Sikandar Shah.

A Wikimedia Commons monument record describes the present dargah structure as 17th–18th century and built in honour of the 14th-century Sikandar Shah. That description concerns the monument's dating; it does not settle every tradition about the shrine or the saint.

Different traditions · building date distinguished
1875CE
05 · DOCUMENTARY RECORD

A management agreement

A registered agreement dated 20 December 1875 is cited in later court records in connection with the hereditary Haqdar families and management of the dargah.

This is a documented management milestone. It is not proof that the shrine itself was founded in 1875.

1920–1931CE
07 · PROPERTY LITIGATION

The hill enters the courts

A property suit was filed in 1920. A 1923 decree recognised distinct property interests over different portions of the hill. The matter later reached the Privy Council, which restored the decree in 1931.

The legal history concerns defined property rights and boundaries. It should not be reduced to a declaration that the entire hill belonged to one religious community.

Court record · property rights
1996CE
08 · DEEPAM PRACTICE

The customary lamp location

Earlier litigation over the Karthigai Deepam addressed the customary lighting location near the Uchipillaiyar Temple and the possibility of other locations subject to conditions. The exact wording of the judgment matters; later parties rely on it in different ways.

Legal interpretation · read the judgment
2025–2026CE
09 · THE PRESENT DISPUTE

The Deepathoon question returns

Petitioners argued that the stone pillar should be used for the Karthigai Deepam. Opposing parties questioned the claimed custom and the pillar's identity, including whether it could be a survey marker. The Madras High Court's 6 January 2026 Division Bench decision permitted the ceremony with safeguards. The State challenged the decision in the Supreme Court.

On 20 November 2026, the Supreme Court allowed a one-time arrangement for 24 November, while making clear that the interim permission did not establish a customary, proprietary or religious right. The lamp was lit at the customary Deepa Mandapam, not at Deepathoon. On 25 November, the Supreme Court was informed of the non-occurrence; a formal contempt application was indicated as the route for further proceedings.

Interim order · underlying issues remain open
*Dating note: Some catalogues date particular Tamil-Brahmi cave-bed inscriptions to the 2nd–1st centuries BCE. The 1965–66 ASI epigraphic study describes the cave inscriptions more broadly as evidence of ascetic use in the early centuries CE. We retain this range rather than forcing a single date onto every inscription.

THE QUESTION EVERYONE ASKS

So, who came first?

The answer depends on what is being compared: physical remains, literary traditions, buildings or documented institutions.

OLDEST PHYSICAL EVIDENCE

Ascetic cave use

The oldest surviving physical evidence identified in the sources is the cave-bed and Tamil-Brahmi inscription record, associated with ascetic use and particularly Jain remains.

ANCIENT LITERARY TRADITION

Murugan at Parankunram

Tamil literary works, including the Paripadal, describe Parankunram as a Murugan pilgrimage centre; the dating of the relevant verses is debated. This tradition predates the surviving Pandya cave-temple inscriptions, though the dating of the poems is debated.

DATED ARCHITECTURE

Pandya cave temple

The rock-cut temple foundation and consecration are recorded in inscriptions dated to 773–774 CE. This is a building milestone, not the beginning of all Murugan worship.

LATER MONUMENTAL LAYER

The hilltop dargah

The extant dargah monument is described in a Commons monument record as 17th–18th century, honouring the 14th-century Sikandar Shah. Other shrine traditions and saint identifications require separate examination.

The evidence supports ancient Jain-associated ascetic use and an ancient Hindu literary association with Murugan. It does not support turning every historical layer into a simple contest over modern ownership or rights. The dargah's precise foundation history remains a separate question.

THE MODERN QUESTION

What is actually disputed?

The present controversy concerns one pillar and a proposed additional lamp-lighting site — not the existence of Karthigai Deepam itself.

What petitioners argue

  • The pillar is a Deepathoon and is suitable for lighting the festival lamp.
  • The additional ceremony should be allowed without interfering with the dargah's lawful access or worship.
  • The claimed religious practice deserves judicial consideration.

What opposing parties argue

  • The established temple practice is to light the lamp at the customary Deepa Mandapam.
  • The pillar's historical purpose and the claimed custom have not been conclusively established.
  • Property rights, access, archaeological protection and public order must be respected.

The Supreme Court's interim position

The 20 November 2026 permission was expressly a one-time arrangement. It did not determine the pillar's original purpose, ownership, customary status or permanent religious entitlement. Further arguments were listed for January 2027.

QUICK ANSWERS

Questions readers ask

Short answers with the historical distinctions kept intact.

How old is Thiruparankundram?

The hill has archaeological and literary evidence reaching back more than two millennia. Some Tamil-Brahmi cave-bed inscriptions are catalogued to the 2nd–1st centuries BCE, while early Tamil literature also describes the hill as a Murugan pilgrimage centre. These are different kinds of evidence, and dates vary by record.

When was the Thiruparankundram rock-cut temple built?

Two Pandya inscriptions record the excavation of a rock-cut cave temple and the consecration of Sambhu (Shiva) in 773–774 CE. The complex also contained other shrines and Subrahmanya imagery. This date should not be used as the construction date of the entire present temple complex.

When was the Sikkandar Badusha Dargah established?

The exact foundation date is not conclusively established by the sources used here. A Wikimedia Commons monument record describes the extant structure as 17th–18th century and links it to 14th-century Sikandar Shah. A 2025 Madras High Court judgment records differing traditions about the saint's identity. The date of a saint, tomb and present building should not be conflated.

Who came first at Thiruparankundram?

The oldest surviving physical evidence is the cave-bed and Tamil-Brahmi inscription record associated with ascetic use, particularly Jain remains. Murugan's association with Parankunram is also ancient and appears in Tamil literature. The evidence does not justify a simplistic answer that one present-day community alone was the first to use the entire hill.

Did the Supreme Court permanently allow lighting the lamp at Deepathoon?

No. On 20 November 2026, the Supreme Court permitted a one-time arrangement for 24 November with strict safeguards. It expressly left the questions of custom, ownership and permanent rights undecided.
