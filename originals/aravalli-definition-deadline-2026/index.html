VRIDHI ORIGINALS · LANDSCAPE · LAW · ECOLOGY

The Aravallis are more than hills. The Supreme Court's definition deadline is now days away.

A line on a map can determine which land is recognised, which protections apply and where mining rules operate. The dispute is not only about the height of a hill—it is about how an ancient, connected landscape is understood.

VRIDHI ORIGINALS · 25 NOVEMBER 2026 · 8 MIN READ · EXPLAINER

In five days, a Supreme Court-appointed expert committee is due to submit a report that could shape how the Aravalli Hills and Ranges are defined and demarcated. The deadline is 30 November 2026. The Court has said it will take up the matter again on 2 December.

That timetable makes this more than a technical debate. The definition used for a mountain range can affect how land is mapped, how mining activity is regulated and which ecological connections are recognised. A hill may be visible to a village, form part of a local watershed and support scrub or forest habitat—yet still be treated differently depending on the criteria used to draw an official boundary.

Why is the definition being reconsidered?

The dispute concerns a uniform definition of the Aravalli Hills and Ranges for regulatory purposes, particularly in the context of mining. In November 2025, the Supreme Court accepted a committee-recommended operational definition based on a landform rising at least 100 metres above the local relief, along with a rule for grouping hills into ranges.

That approach quickly drew scrutiny. Questions were raised about what a height-based threshold might leave outside the mapped Aravalli system, and whether geology, ecology, hydrology and landscape continuity were adequately captured by a single terrain measure. The Court subsequently paused implementation and sought independent expert examination rather than treating the issue as settled.

What did the Supreme Court do next?

On 25 May 2026, the Court constituted a five-member High-Powered Committee to examine the earlier report and the wider questions surrounding definition and demarcation. The committee includes experts in forestry, geological mapping and botany, with special invitees in environmental and landscape disciplines. The Court also directed the panel to invite views from stakeholders, including states, local communities, conservation groups and mining interests.

The 7 September order: no further extension

On 7 September 2026, the committee sought six more months, until February 2027, to complete its report. The Supreme Court rejected that request and directed it to submit the comprehensive report by 30 November 2026, stating that no further extension would be granted. The Court also said interim reports could be submitted on specific issues and directed that mining leases should not be formally renewed and mining activity should not be permitted until further orders, consistent with its earlier directions.

As of 25 November 2026, the 30 November deadline has not yet passed. Vridhi has not located a published final report in the sources reviewed for this article. The next listed hearing is 2 December. The committee's report and the Court's subsequent directions may change the position, so this story should be updated when those documents become available.

Why a mountain range is not just a row of peaks

The Aravalli system is commonly described as extending across parts of Gujarat, Rajasthan, Haryana and the National Capital Region. But an ecological landscape includes more than high, dramatic summits. It can include lower ridges, slopes, valleys, rocky outcrops, scrub, forest patches, seasonal drainage lines and the spaces that connect habitats.

Those connections matter. A ridge can influence how rainwater moves. A valley can hold soil and vegetation. A patch of scrub may provide habitat or act as a stepping stone between larger areas. If mapping focuses only on a narrow physical feature, it may fail to represent the wider ecological functions that depend on continuity.

This does not mean every parcel of land in the broader region should automatically receive identical legal status. It means the criteria need to be transparent, scientifically defensible and explicit about what they include, what they exclude and why.

What should the expert committee examine?

The Court's May order asked for a comprehensive evaluation, not a quick redrawing of a contour line. Relevant questions include geological structure and mapping; biodiversity and ecological connectivity; water systems and catchments; the areas that could be excluded under competing definitions; the cumulative and long-term consequences of mining; and the interests and rights of people who live and work in the landscape.

That last point is essential. The region is not an empty map. It contains villages, farms, workers, settlements, conservation areas and competing economic claims. A credible assessment must listen to affected communities while also examining the ecological consequences that may cross administrative boundaries.

What happens after 30 November?

The committee's report is expected by the Court's deadline. The Supreme Court is scheduled to hear the matter on 2 December 2026. The report will inform the Court; it does not itself mean that a final legal definition has automatically taken effect. The Court will have to consider the material and issue further directions.

What is confirmed—and what is not
Confirmed: the expert committee was constituted on 25 May; the Court's 7 September order set 30 November as the report deadline and 2 December as the next hearing. Not confirmed as of this article's publication date: the final committee report or a final, settled definition following that report.

The boundary is a decision about the landscape

The Aravalli debate shows how scientific classifications become public decisions. A definition may look like a technical exercise, but it influences what is counted, what is protected and what can be altered. The strongest outcome will be one that explains its evidence, makes its mapping reproducible and considers the landscape as a connected system—not simply as a collection of isolated hilltops.
