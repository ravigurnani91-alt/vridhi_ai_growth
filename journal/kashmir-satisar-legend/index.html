VRIDHI ORIGINALS · CHAPTER 01

Was Kashmir
once a giant lake?

An ancient story speaks of Satisar, a vast lake ringed by mountains. The land beneath that legend holds a geological story of its own—and traces of people who lived here 5,000 years ago.

KASHMIR · MYTHOLOGY · GEOLOGY · ARCHAEOLOGY   /   8 MIN READ

Before there were cities, highways or houseboats, Kashmir's origin story begins with water. The Nilamata Purana remembers a lake called Satisar. Archaeology, meanwhile, takes us to a settlement where people built homes inside the earth around 3000 BCE.

Close your eyes and picture the Kashmir Valley before the roads, towns and gardens we know today. No traffic curling through Srinagar. No shikaras crossing Dal Lake. No orchards climbing the slopes. Just a vast bowl of mountains—and, in the story Kashmir has told about its own beginning, water stretching across the valley.

That ancient lake had a name: Satisar. And the story of how it disappeared is stranger, more dramatic and more deeply woven into Kashmir's cultural memory than a simple line in a history book can convey.

1. Imagine a Kashmir with no land in sight

The Nilamata Purana, an important Sanskrit text associated with Kashmir, opens a window into a world in which the valley was once a great lake called Satisaras, usually shortened to Satisar—the Lake of Sati. Mountains rose around it. The waters belonged to a landscape inhabited in the story by Nagas, powerful serpent-associated beings who occupy an important place in Kashmir's older traditions.

At the heart of this origin story is the sage Kashyapa. The narrative remembers a land that could not yet be lived in as the valley we recognise. Before villages, fields and places of worship could flourish, the waters had to recede. The story gives that transformation a divine cast of characters—and a formidable obstacle.

This was not a story invented for tourists. Kashmir's lake-origin tradition was important enough to be recalled centuries later by Kalhana, the 12th-century author of the Rajatarangini. His chronicle begins by looking back towards the legendary lake and the sage Kashyapa.

2. The one who lived in the water

Now the story takes a darker turn. The lake is associated with Jalodbhava—a name commonly understood as “water-born”. In the traditional account, the being is protected by the waters and becomes a danger to those around the lake. The problem is not simply that the valley is underwater. It is that the water has become a refuge for something that cannot easily be defeated.

Kashyapa seeks divine help. In the version retold in the historical and textual tradition, Vishnu calls upon Balabhadra, also known as Balarama. With his plough, Balarama opens an outlet through the mountains. The water begins to escape. Once Jalodbhava is no longer protected by the lake, he can be overcome.

Imagine the image at the centre of the legend: water finding a way out through a mountain barrier, the lake falling, and a new landscape appearing where there had once been open water. The story turns a change in the land into a memorable act of divine intervention.

A story about the birth of a homelandFor generations of readers, Satisar offered more than an explanation for a landscape. It connected Kashmir's mountains, water, sacred beings and human settlement in one origin story.

3. And then the ground itself tells a story

Here is the detail that makes you pause. The Kashmir Valley really does preserve evidence of an ancient and very different environment. Across parts of the basin are thick deposits called Karewas—layers of sediment built up over long stretches of geological time.

Think of them as pages in a book written by the landscape. Their layers preserve clues about changing environments: periods when water, rivers, lakes, climate and the shifting Himalayan terrain shaped the valley. Scientists study the material in those layers to reconstruct parts of Kashmir's deep past.

So, was Satisar a real lake? The careful answer is that geology supports the existence of ancient lake environments in the Kashmir basin. It does not prove that the events happened exactly as the Purana describes them, or that a particular divine act drained a single lake at a known date.

That distinction does not make the story less interesting. It gives us two remarkable ways of looking at the same landscape: one preserved through sacred narrative, the other recovered from rocks and sediment. We can place them beside each other without pretending they are the same kind of evidence.

4. Meet a family from 5,000 years ago

Travel in your imagination to a place northeast of Srinagar called Burzahom. Around 3000 BCE, people were making a life here. They had no written biographies for us to read. We cannot know what they called themselves, what stories they told their children at night, or what they thought when winter closed in.

But the ground has kept some of their story.

Some homes were dug down into the earth. Archaeologists found pits that were narrow near the top and wider below—an ingenious way to create a sheltered living space in a cold climate. Evidence of post-holes suggests that the openings could be covered with a roof or superstructure. Hearths and other features offer clues to daily domestic life.

Picture the practical rhythm of a day: someone tending a fire, someone shaping a stone tool, someone preparing food, children learning by watching the adults. That scene is an imaginative reconstruction, not a diary of a particular family—but it is grounded in the kinds of objects and structures archaeologists have uncovered.

Over the centuries, life at Burzahom changed. Later phases show above-ground structures, pottery and evidence of farming. People used tools made from stone and bone, hunted animals and cultivated crops including wheat, barley and lentils. They were not simply passing through. They were adapting to this place.

5. Were they the first Kashmiris?

It is tempting to give the people of Burzahom the title of “the first Kashmiris”. But history is rarely that neat. Burzahom gives us one of the clearest windows into settled Neolithic life in the valley; it does not prove that nobody lived here before them.

The Archaeological Survey of India's excavation report discusses earlier Palaeolithic stone-tool finds from parts of the valley, including surface discoveries. It also notes the limits of the evidence available to the researchers: the earliest traces are difficult to turn into a complete story of where people lived, how long they stayed or what communities they belonged to. Some tool finds have been tentatively assigned to much earlier periods, but those interpretations need care.

So we cannot name the first person who stood in this valley. We cannot confidently point to one tribe and say, “These were the first.” The earliest people left no written names. What they left behind are fragments: a tool, a dwelling pit, a hearth, a bone, a layer of earth.

Somewhere in this valley, thousands of years ago, a child grew up without ever knowing that one day people would dig into the ground to find traces of their home.

6. Three pasts, one extraordinary valley

By now, Kashmir's beginning looks less like a single date and more like a set of stories layered over one another. The Nilamata Purana remembers a sacred lake and the powers associated with its transformation. Geology reads the valley through sediments and the long history of the Himalayan basin. Archaeology brings us close to people whose homes and tools survived long after their names disappeared.

We should not force these records to say the same thing. The legend is a cultural and religious narrative. The sediments are geological evidence. The dwellings and tools are archaeological evidence. Each has its own way of telling us something about this place.

And this is only the beginning.If the valley's oldest story begins with Satisar, the next question is irresistible: who were the Nagas in Kashmir's early traditions, and what place did they hold in the world described by the Nilamata Purana?

Still curious? Quick answers

Was Kashmir once covered by water?

The Kashmir basin preserves extensive ancient sediments, including deposits associated with lake environments. The scale and timing of past water bodies are geological questions; they do not establish every event in the Satisar legend.

What does Satisar mean?

Satisar is commonly rendered as the Lake of Sati. It is the great primordial lake in the Kashmir origin narrative preserved in the Nilamata Purana.

Who drained Satisar according to the legend?

In the version summarised by Aurel Stein, Vishnu calls upon Balabhadra, or Balarama, who opens an outlet through the mountains with his ploughshare.

Who lived in Kashmir 5,000 years ago?

Neolithic communities lived at Burzahom from around 3000 BCE. Archaeology has not identified their personal names or established that they were the first humans in the valley.

Were the people of Burzahom part of a modern community?

The available archaeological evidence does not justify assigning the Neolithic inhabitants a present-day religious, ethnic or national identity. Their material culture should be studied on its own terms.
