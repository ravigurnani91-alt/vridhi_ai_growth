The King Who Built a Sun Temple in the Mountains

Lalitaditya, the Karkota dynasty and Martand Sun Temple: the story behind one of Kashmir's most extraordinary surviving monuments.

KASHMIR · HISTORY · CULTURE · RESEARCH   /   7 MIN READ

On a plateau above the Kashmir Valley stands a ruined temple that still commands the landscape. Who built it—and what did a king want the rising sun to say about his power?

A king remembered in stoneA temple built for the sunWhat the ruins can tell usBeyond the legend of conquest

1. A king remembered in stone

The Karkota dynasty shaped early medieval Kashmir. Its best-known ruler, Lalitaditya Muktapida, is remembered in Kalhana's Rajatarangini as a powerful king whose ambitions reached far beyond the valley. The chronicle describes campaigns and an expansive realm, but its grandest territorial claims should be treated critically rather than repeated as a verified map of conquest.

What can still be seen is a different kind of evidence: architecture. The Martand Sun Temple, associated with Lalitaditya and generally dated to the eighth century, remains one of Kashmir's most striking historic monuments.

2. A temple built for the sun

The complex stands near Mattan in the Anantnag area. Its central shrine, surrounding colonnade and enclosed courtyard create a powerful sense of scale. Even in ruin, the design makes the visitor look inward towards the sanctuary and outward towards the mountain landscape.

The temple's dedication to the Sun gives the setting another layer. Sun worship has a long history across South Asia, and Martand's architecture expresses royal patronage through a monumental sacred space.

3. What the ruins can tell us

A monument is not only a beautiful photograph. Its plan, stonework, setting and surviving fragments help historians study architecture, patronage and the resources a ruler could mobilise. The Archaeological Survey of India protects the site; the Government of India has confirmed its protected status and ASI care in a parliamentary answer.

Its ruinous condition should not tempt us into inventing one simple explanation for its destruction. The history of the monument's decline is complex and should be investigated through evidence, not slogans.

4. Beyond the legend of conquest

Lalitaditya's reputation is inseparable from Kalhana's account. But the temple gives us something tangible to examine alongside the chronicle. It tells us that early medieval Kashmir could support ambitious architectural projects and that kings used sacred construction to make power visible.

Next time you see a photograph of Martand, look beyond the dramatic arches. Imagine the people who quarried, moved, carved and assembled the stone—and the generations who continued to give the place meaning.

Martand is not just a ruin. It is surviving evidence of the ambition, craftsmanship and sacred world of early medieval Kashmir.

Questions readers are asking

Who built the Martand Sun Temple?

The temple is traditionally attributed to Lalitaditya Muktapida of the Karkota dynasty and is generally dated to the eighth century.

Where is Martand Sun Temple?

It is located near Mattan in the Anantnag district of Kashmir.

Is Martand protected by the ASI?

Yes. The Ministry of Culture confirmed in a 2023 parliamentary answer that Martand Sun Temple is a protected monument under ASI care and maintenance.
