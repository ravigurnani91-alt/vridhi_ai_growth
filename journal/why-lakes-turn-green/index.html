Why Do Lakes Turn Bright Green? The Science Behind an Algal Bloom

A green lake may look like a harmless seasonal colour change. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it signals an ecological imbalance that deserves attention. The difference lies beneath the surface.

VRIDHI JOURNAL · 20 NOVEMBER 2026 · 5 MIN READ

When a lake changes from blue or brown to vivid green, the colour is often caused by microscopic organisms suspended in the water. Algae and cyanobacteria contain pigments that absorb some wavelengths of light and reflect others. When their populations grow quickly, the water can take on a green paint-like appearance. Scientists call a rapid increase in algae or cyanobacteria an algal bloom.

What makes algae multiply so quickly?

Algae need light, water, carbon and nutrients. In many freshwater systems, the nutrients phosphorus and nitrogen can become the limiting ingredients. When excess nutrients enter a lake through untreated sewage, fertiliser runoff, animal waste or eroding soil, they can help algae grow much faster than normal. Warm temperatures, long sunny days and relatively still water can make conditions even more favourable.

Is every green lake dangerous?

No. Algae are a normal and essential part of aquatic food webs. The concern is a dense bloom, especially one dominated by toxin-producing cyanobacteria. Some cyanobacterial blooms can produce toxins that affect people, pets, livestock and wildlife. You cannot reliably identify a toxic bloom by colour, smell or appearance alone. Avoid contact with suspicious scum or discoloured water and follow local public-health advice.

Why does the bloom sometimes disappear?

A change in wind, temperature, water mixing or nutrient availability can break up a bloom. When large quantities of algae die and decompose, bacteria consume dissolved oxygen. This can contribute to low-oxygen conditions, stressing or killing fish and other aquatic organisms. The lake may look clearer later, but the underlying nutrient problem can remain.

How can a lake be brought back into balance?

The durable solution is usually to reduce the nutrients entering the waterbody. That can mean improving sewage collection and treatment, controlling stormwater, managing fertiliser use, restoring wetlands and vegetated buffers, and preventing dumping. Aeration or chemical treatment may be used in specific cases, but they do not replace catchment-level pollution control.

What should residents look for?

Record the location and date, note whether the water has surface scum or an unusual smell, and report it to the relevant municipal or pollution-control authority. Do not enter the water, allow pets to drink it or attempt to collect samples without suitable guidance. A photograph is useful for reporting, but laboratory testing is needed to identify organisms and toxins.

The key idea
A green lake is a clue, not a diagnosis. The key questions are what organisms are present, what nutrients are entering the lake, and whether testing finds toxins or oxygen stress.
