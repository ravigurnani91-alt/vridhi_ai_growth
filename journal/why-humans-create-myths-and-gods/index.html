Why Do Human Societies Create Gods, Myths and Origin Stories?

Stories about gods, beginnings and extraordinary beings appear in societies across the world. They are not all the same—and they do more than explain the unknown.

VRIDHI JOURNAL · 20 NOVEMBER 2026 · 5 MIN READ

Every society inherits stories about where the world came from, why people suffer, how order should be maintained and what happens after death. Some stories centre on gods, ancestors, spirits or heroic figures. Others describe animals, landscapes or cosmic forces. Together, myths form part of how communities organise memory, identity and meaning.

Stories make the world understandable

Before modern scientific methods, communities used narrative to interpret storms, seasons, disease, birth, death and the movement of celestial bodies. A myth could connect an event to a moral lesson, a ritual or a shared account of the past. That does not mean every traditional story was intended as a literal scientific explanation.

Myths carry values and social rules

Stories can model courage, generosity, restraint, loyalty, justice or the consequences of pride. They may legitimise institutions and hierarchies too. A careful reading asks whose values a story promotes, who gets to tell it and how interpretations change over time.

Place and landscape become part of the story

Mountains, rivers, forests, caves and cities often appear in sacred narratives. Such stories can strengthen attachment to a landscape and shape how it is protected, used or contested. The same place may hold different meanings for different communities.

Stories travel and change

Trade, migration, conquest, intermarriage and translation move stories between languages and regions. Motifs can resemble one another without proving that one culture simply copied another. Similar human questions can also produce similar narrative patterns independently.

Respect belief while studying evidence

A religious belief is meaningful to its adherents, but historical claims about dates, origins or events require appropriate evidence. Folklore, theology, archaeology, textual history and personal faith are different kinds of knowledge. Good writing identifies which kind it is discussing rather than treating them as interchangeable.

The key idea
Myths are not merely failed science or old entertainment. They are cultural records—complex, changing stories through which people express identity, values and relationships with the world.
