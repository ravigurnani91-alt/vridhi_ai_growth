The Man Who Tried to Record Kashmir's Entire Past

In the 12th century, Kalhana wrote the Rajatarangini, a chronicle of Kashmir's kings. Discover his sources, his method and where legend meets history.

KASHMIR · HISTORY · CULTURE · RESEARCH   /   7 MIN READ

Nearly nine centuries ago, a Kashmiri writer set out to tell the story of his land—not just one king, but generations of rulers, mistakes, ambition and memory. His name was Kalhana.

1. A writer who wanted to look backwards

Kalhana composed the Rajatarangini in Sanskrit in the middle of the twelfth century, usually dated to around 1148–1150 CE. The title is often translated as “River of Kings”. The work is arranged in eight books, or tarangas, and carries Kashmir's story from legendary beginnings to the political world close to the author's own time.

Think about the scale of the task. Kalhana was writing before modern archives, photography or archaeological departments. He had to assemble the past from materials that survived in his own world.

2. He did not rely on one source

Kalhana refers to earlier chronicles and traditions. Scholars have also noted his use of inscriptions, land grants, temple records, coins and other materials. He evaluates earlier accounts and criticises some writers. That effort to compare sources is one reason the Rajatarangini is so important to the study of Kashmir.

But it is not a modern history textbook. It is a Sanskrit literary work, written in verse, shaped by the conventions and beliefs of its time. Its earlier sections include legendary material and chronology that cannot simply be accepted as literal fact.

3. Not every page has the same reliability

The closer Kalhana gets to the political events of his own era, the more detailed and historically useful the narrative becomes. The earliest books mix dynastic memory, myth, moral reflection and traditional chronology. Later portions offer much richer accounts of rulers and political struggles.

This is not a flaw unique to Kalhana. It is a reminder that historical sources must be read with questions: Who wrote this? When? What sources did they use? What could they know? What did they want readers to remember?

4. A river that kept flowing

Kalhana's chronicle did not end Kashmir's historical writing. Later authors, including Jonaraja and others, continued the tradition and carried the narrative forward. The title itself became a way of imagining history as a river: rulers arrive, reign and disappear, while the story keeps moving.

And that brings us back to the beginning of this series. The Satisar legend, the people of Burzahom, the Buddhist centres, Martand and Abhinavagupta all belong to different kinds of evidence and memory. Kalhana helps us see how Kashmir itself tried to gather many of those pasts into one long account.

A chronicle is not the past itself. It is a writer's attempt to gather the past—and Kalhana left us one of Kashmir's most important attempts.

Questions readers are asking

When did Kalhana write the Rajatarangini?

The work is generally dated to around 1148–1150 CE, in the twelfth century.

What does Rajatarangini mean?

The title is commonly translated as “River of Kings”.

Is everything in the Rajatarangini historically accurate?

No. Its earlier sections include legends and uncertain chronology; later sections are generally more useful for reconstructing events closer to Kalhana's own time.

How many books are in the Rajatarangini?

It is divided into eight tarangas, or books/cantos.
