Who Were the Nagas of Ancient Kashmir?

Were Kashmir's Nagas serpents, human communities or sacred guardians? Follow the Nilamata Purana, ancient place traditions and what scholars can—and cannot—say.

KASHMIR · HISTORY · CULTURE · RESEARCH   /   7 MIN READ

A serpent at a spring. A guardian beneath the earth. A people whose names appear across an ancient valley. In Kashmir's oldest stories, the Nagas are everywhere. But who—or what—were they?

1. A valley full of Naga names

In Chapter 1, we met Satisar: the lake at the centre of Kashmir's origin story. Now look closer at the world around that lake. The Nilamata Purana repeatedly brings us back to the Nagas. They are not a passing detail. They are woven into the sacred geography of the valley—its waters, places of worship and festivals.

One figure stands out: Nila, the Naga king associated with the valley's traditions. The text preserves accounts of Naga communities and rituals, and gives us a glimpse of how people understood the relationship between landscape, divinity and everyday life.

2. So… were they snakes?

The word Naga can refer to a serpent or serpent-associated divine being in Indian religious traditions. But in the Nilamata Purana, the references are not always that simple. Some passages describe serpent-like beings; others appear to refer to groups or human-like communities. Modern scholarship has cautioned that the meaning shifts with context and remains difficult to pin down.

That uncertainty is the story—not a gap to fill with a confident guess. We cannot responsibly declare that every Naga reference means a literal snake, or that every one names a historical tribe.

3. The spring, the serpent and the sacred place

Across South Asian traditions, springs, lakes and rivers have often been associated with Nagas. In Kashmir, where water shapes settlement and survival, that connection feels especially powerful. The Nilamata Purana links Naga names with sacred places and watercourses. It also records festivals and observances tied to the valley's seasonal and ritual life.

Read this as a map of meaning: a spring is not merely a source of water; it can also be a place with a story, a guardian and a community's memory. The text lets us see how sacred geography helped people make sense of the land.

4. What can we actually conclude?

The Nilamata Purana is an important source for Kashmir's religious and social traditions, not a census or a contemporary archaeological report. Its Naga references are valuable evidence for the cultural world the text preserves. They do not, by themselves, settle the historical identity of every group named in it.

So we leave the question open: perhaps different Naga traditions accumulated over centuries, carrying memories of water worship, local communities and divine guardianship. That is a research possibility, not a proven single explanation.

The Nagas are not a puzzle we should solve by force. They are a clue to how ancient Kashmir connected people, water, place and belief.

Questions readers are asking

Who is Nila in Kashmir mythology?

Nila is remembered as a Naga king in the Kashmir traditions preserved by the Nilamata Purana.

Were the Nagas real people?

The texts use Naga in different ways, including serpent-associated beings and references that may indicate human communities. The exact historical identity is unresolved.

Why are Nagas linked with water?

Naga traditions across South Asia often connect serpent beings with springs, lakes and rivers. The Nilamata Purana also links Naga names with Kashmir's sacred geography.
