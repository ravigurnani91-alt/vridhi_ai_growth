When Giant Stones Rose in Kashmir

After the pit-dwelling world of Burzahom, huge standing stones marked a new chapter. Explore Kashmir's megalithic remains and evidence of wider connections.

KASHMIR · HISTORY · CULTURE · RESEARCH   /   7 MIN READ

Long after the first pit homes at Burzahom, people raised enormous stones on the plateau. Some still stand. Who placed them there—and what were they meant to remember?

1. A different skyline

At Burzahom, the story does not stop with pit dwellings. The archaeological sequence moves through later phases, including a Megalithic period associated with the erection of large stones called menhirs. The Archaeological Survey of India's site record notes eleven megaliths, five still erect and others fallen.

Imagine approaching the plateau and seeing these stones rise above the ground. They are not the walls of a house or the remains of an ordinary workshop. They mark a different kind of human effort: moving, placing and keeping large stones in a landscape.

2. What were they for?

UNESCO describes the megaliths as probably commemorative, but the precise purpose of individual stones is not established by their size alone. Across different regions, megaliths have been associated with memorials, burials and other forms of social marking. That wider comparison can guide questions, but it cannot replace evidence from Burzahom itself.

Were they markers for the dead? Did they signal a shared memory or a place of importance? We should treat these as questions, not settled answers.

3. A community changing over time

The material culture changed too. UNESCO's account describes wheel-made pottery, metal objects and continued use of some stone and bone tools in the Megalithic phase. The site then records an early historic phase with mud-brick structures, pottery and metal objects, dated in the description to the third–fourth centuries CE.

That long sequence is what makes Burzahom remarkable: it is not one moment sealed under the soil, but a place where ways of living changed over a very long span.

4. Kashmir and the wider world

The finds at Burzahom—including beads and evidence related to crops—have been used to discuss contacts between the valley, the Himalayan hinterland and regions beyond. UNESCO's nomination describes Kashmir as a bridge between mountain regions and wider networks. That is a useful way to think about the geography, provided we do not turn it into a claim that one clearly mapped route or one single migration explains everything.

Mountains can divide. They can also connect. The archaeological record suggests that people in prehistoric Kashmir were part of a wider world of movement and exchange.

The stones are still there. Their builders are not. The challenge is to read what survives without pretending we know every reason they raised them.

Questions readers are asking

How many megaliths are at Burzahom?

The ASI site record notes eleven megaliths, five erect and the others fallen.

What is a menhir?

A menhir is a large upright stone, often erected as part of a megalithic tradition.

Were the Burzahom stones definitely graves?

Their exact function is not fully established. UNESCO describes them as probably commemorative; specific interpretations need archaeological context.
