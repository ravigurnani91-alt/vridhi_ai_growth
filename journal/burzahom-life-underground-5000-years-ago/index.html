The People Who Built Their Homes Underground

Inside Burzahom, where Neolithic families dug homes into the earth, made tools from bone and stone, and adapted to Kashmir's harsh seasons.

KASHMIR · HISTORY · CULTURE · RESEARCH   /   7 MIN READ

Imagine winter arriving in the valley. Outside, the air bites. Inside a home dug into the earth, a fire glows. Five thousand years ago, this was one way people made a life in Kashmir.

1. A home below the ground

At Burzahom, northeast of Srinagar, archaeologists uncovered something that changes the way you picture prehistoric life: homes dug down into the soil. Many were circular or oval, narrow near the top and wider at the base. Some were several metres deep.

These were not random holes. Their shapes, steps, traces of hearths and post-holes help archaeologists understand how people used the spaces and protected themselves from the cold. The earth itself became part of the architecture.

2. A day in a Neolithic home

We cannot know the name of the person who lit a particular hearth. But the objects left behind bring daily life into focus. Stone tools helped people cut, scrape and shape. Bone tools included needles, awls and points. Hearths suggest cooking and warmth. Storage features point to the careful business of keeping food and supplies.

Picture a family sharing a meal, repairing clothing, preparing tools and watching the weather. It is an imagined scene, but the tools and structures that make it plausible were recovered from the site.

3. They were not only hunters

The archaeological record points to a changing economy. People hunted animals, but evidence from plant remains and tools also indicates cultivation. Wheat, barley and lentils appear in the site's Neolithic sequence. Over time, pottery and different forms of buildings became part of life here.

Burzahom was not a frozen village. It changed across centuries, and the people living there adapted their homes, tools and ways of getting food.

4. A place connected beyond the valley

UNESCO's Tentative List description notes that lentil cultivation and material finds at Burzahom help researchers investigate connections between Kashmir, the Himalayan hinterland and regions farther west and north. The evidence does not give us a neat map of a single trade road. It does show that prehistoric Kashmir should not be imagined as an isolated pocket cut off from the wider world.

The people of Burzahom left no written memoir. Their homes, hearths and tools are the closest thing we have to a window into their lives.

Questions readers are asking

How old is Burzahom?

The UNESCO Tentative List describes a cultural sequence from around 3000 BCE to 1000 BCE, covering Neolithic, Megalithic and early historic phases.

Why did people live in pit dwellings?

The pits were dug into the soil and may have helped provide shelter in a harsh climate. Post-holes suggest that some had protective superstructures.

What did people eat?

Evidence indicates cultivation of wheat, barley and lentils, alongside hunting and animal use. The exact diet varied over time.
