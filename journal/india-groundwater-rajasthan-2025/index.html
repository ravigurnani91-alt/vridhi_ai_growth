EARTH · WATER · INDIA

India extracts 60% of its groundwater. Rajasthan extracts 133% of its annual recharge.

A national average can sound manageable while entire regions are using more groundwater than they can replenish. The 2025 figures reveal why the local picture matters far more than one headline percentage.

VRIDHI EARTH · 25 NOVEMBER 2026 · 7 MIN READ · DATA EXPLAINER

India's groundwater story has two numbers that seem to pull in opposite directions. At the national level, annual extraction is assessed at 60.63% of the extractable resource. In Rajasthan, the corresponding state figure is approximately 133%. One is an average across a vast and varied country; the other is a warning about a state where extraction, in aggregate, exceeds the annual resource considered extractable.

Neither number tells you how much water is in the well outside a particular home. But together they explain why national averages can conceal local stress—and why groundwater policy has to be read block by block.

What does 60.63% actually measure?

The Central Ground Water Board's 2025 assessment estimates India's annual groundwater recharge at 448.52 billion cubic metres (BCM). After accounting for natural discharge, annual extractable groundwater resources are estimated at 407.75 BCM. Annual extraction for irrigation, industry and domestic use is assessed at 247.22 BCM.

The stage of groundwater extraction is calculated by comparing annual extraction with annual extractable groundwater resources. It is not the percentage of all groundwater stored underground that remains. It is not a measure of how full aquifers are, nor a guarantee that water is available in every district.

The national average hides 730 over-exploited assessment units

India's 2025 assessment covered 6,762 administrative groundwater assessment units, generally blocks, tehsils or talukas. Of these, 730 were classified as over-exploited, 201 as critical, 758 as semi-critical, 4,946 as safe and 127 as saline.

That distribution is the important context. A national figure can improve while a particular block remains under severe pressure. The categories are based on the assessment methodology and annual resource balance; they should not be mistaken for a live reading of every borewell.

Rajasthan: when extraction crosses the annual resource

A Lok Sabha reply based on the 2025 assessment gives Rajasthan annual groundwater recharge of 12.87 BCM and annual extraction of 17.10 BCM. Dividing extraction by recharge gives roughly 133%. In the same reply, 213 of Rajasthan's 302 assessment blocks—70.53%—were classified as over-exploited, while 23 were critical.

Barmer offers a district-level example in the same parliamentary answer: annual recharge was reported at 0.385 BCM and extraction at 0.469 BCM. These figures are not a substitute for block-level field measurements, but they show why the state average alone is not enough.

Read the denominator carefully. The national 60.63% uses annual extractable resources (407.75 BCM) as its denominator. The Rajasthan 133% estimate above uses the reported annual recharge (12.87 BCM), because that is the pair of state figures provided in the cited Lok Sabha answer. They are useful warning indicators, but they are not strictly identical calculations. Rajasthan's official state assessment should be consulted for the comparable state stage-of-extraction figure.

Why can recharge and extraction move in different directions?

Groundwater is replenished unevenly. Rainfall intensity and timing, soil, geology, land cover, surface storage and the condition of local catchments all influence recharge. Extraction is shaped by irrigation demand, cropping patterns, pumping technology, urban growth and industrial use. A place may receive monsoon rain yet still withdraw water faster than its aquifers recover.

Recharge is also not a simple annual refill button. Water may take time to move through soil and rock; some rainfall runs off; and water quality can limit what is usable. A rising recharge estimate at national scale does not mean every aquifer is recovering.

What should citizens and planners look at?

For a district or block, the useful questions are specific: What is the stage of extraction? Is the unit safe, semi-critical, critical or over-exploited? How have pre- and post-monsoon water levels changed over several years? What is the quality of the water? Which crops and industries account for demand? Are local lakes, johads, tanks and catchments functioning as recharge systems—or have they been disconnected by construction and pollution?

Those questions connect groundwater science to everyday life. A falling water table can mean deeper borewells, higher pumping costs, more energy use and unequal access. The consequences are not distributed evenly: households with deeper wells or more capital can adapt longer than those who cannot afford another borewell.

The real story is local

India's national groundwater picture is neither uniformly safe nor uniformly collapsing. The 2025 assessment shows a majority of units in the safe category, alongside hundreds of over-exploited units. Rajasthan's figures make the unevenness especially visible. The responsible conclusion is not that one number explains the country; it is that groundwater security has to be measured and managed at the scale where people actually draw water.
