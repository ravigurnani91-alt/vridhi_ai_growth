Kanishka, Buddhist Scholars and the Kashmir Connection

The Kushan age connected Kashmir to a vast world of trade and ideas. Explore Kanishka, Buddhist learning and the debated Fourth Buddhist Council.

KASHMIR · HISTORY · CULTURE · RESEARCH   /   7 MIN READ

Imagine scholars travelling across mountain routes with manuscripts, arguments and ideas. In the Kushan age, Kashmir became part of a much wider intellectual world.

1. A new power in the north

Several centuries after Ashoka, the Kushans rose to power across parts of Central and South Asia. Their realm connected regions that today sit across modern national borders. Kashmir's position in the north-western mountains placed it within a landscape of movement, exchange and competing cultural influences.

Kanishka is the best-known Kushan ruler associated with Buddhist patronage. His coins are striking evidence of a diverse religious world: Kushan coinage features a range of divine figures, not a single religious identity.

2. The council remembered in Buddhist history

Buddhist traditions associate Kanishka with a major council in Kashmir, often called the Fourth Buddhist Council in northern Buddhist accounts. The council is linked with the Sarvastivada school and the development of important scholastic literature.

But the details are debated. Sources differ over the location, date, participants and historical setting. The popular claim that the council definitely took place at Harwan near Srinagar should not be presented as settled fact. A tradition can be historically important even when its precise coordinates remain uncertain.

3. A place where ideas travelled

Kashmir's importance did not depend on one royal gathering. Buddhist monasteries, teachers, students and texts connected the valley to wider networks. Later Chinese pilgrims, including Xuanzang, describe Kashmir as an important centre of Buddhist learning. Their accounts offer a different kind of evidence from the much earlier traditions about Ashoka and Kanishka.

Ideas moved with people. A scholar could carry a text across a mountain pass; a student could spend years learning in a monastery; a translation could travel farther than the teacher who first explained it.

4. What survives?

Archaeological sites such as Harwan provide material evidence for Buddhist activity in Kashmir. But identifying a particular structure as the exact venue of Kanishka's council requires more than proximity to Srinagar or a later tradition. The archaeological record, coins, texts and travel accounts each contribute different pieces.

The Kushan chapter is therefore a story of connection: political power, trade, religious patronage and scholarship meeting in the north-west.

Kanishka's council is an important Buddhist tradition. Its exact location and details remain debated; Kashmir's wider Buddhist intellectual history is much better supported.

Questions readers are asking

Did Kanishka hold the Fourth Buddhist Council in Kashmir?

Northern Buddhist traditions associate Kanishka with a major council in Kashmir. Its exact date, location and historical details remain debated.

Was the council held at Harwan?

Harwan is often suggested, but the identification of the site as the definite council venue is not established.

Why was Kashmir important to Buddhist learning?

Literary traditions, archaeology and accounts of later pilgrims indicate that Kashmir became an important centre of Buddhist institutions and scholarship.
