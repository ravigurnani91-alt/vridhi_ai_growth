KATHMANDU, BEYOND THE POSTCARDS / SACRED LANDSCAPES

A STORY OF TWO MASTERS · KATHMANDU VALLEY

Naropa, Tilopa & the caves of Pashupatinath

A scholar leaves the great monasteries of India to find a teacher. Centuries later, stories of their spiritual lineage still echo across Himalayan Buddhist traditions. In Kathmandu, those stories meet one of the valley's oldest sacred landscapes.

VRIDHI FIELD NOTES · 23 NOVEMBER 2026 · 7 MIN READ

Stand beside the Bagmati at Pashupatinath and the valley seems to fold several worlds into one view: temple roofs, stone steps, shrines, monasteries, old trees and the steady movement of people coming here to pray. This is not simply a monument to look at. It is a living sacred neighbourhood.

Among the many religious histories connected with the wider area are Buddhist traditions that remember the Indian teachers Tilopa and Naropa. Their names carry enormous weight in Vajrayana Buddhism. But the story needs one important distinction: the lives of these teachers are documented through Buddhist historical and lineage texts; the precise identification and archaeological dating of particular caves around Pashupatinath are not established by the public sources we could verify.

The history of the teachers is one thing. The local tradition attached to a particular cave is another. A good guide should let both be seen clearly.

The teacher who changed the direction of a scholar

Naropa is remembered as an Indian Buddhist scholar of the eleventh century, associated with the great monastic centres of Nalanda and Vikramashila. In the traditional account, he had reached a position of extraordinary learning when a dakini challenged him: knowing the words of the teachings was not the same as realising their meaning.

That encounter sends Naropa away from institutional scholarship and into a search for the wandering master Tilopa. The stories of that search are dramatic: tests, reversals, hardship and a teacher who refuses to make the path easy. In Kagyu lineage memory, Naropa becomes the disciple through whom Tilopa's teachings pass to later generations, including Marpa the Translator and, eventually, Milarepa.

These biographies are religious life-stories, not modern day-by-day records. They preserve how Buddhist communities understood the teachers and their transmission. Read them as important historical and spiritual traditions, while being careful not to treat every episode as independently verified fact.

NAROPA

An Indian Buddhist master traditionally dated to the 11th century and remembered as a major teacher in the Kagyu lineage.

TILOPA

Naropa's revered teacher, remembered as a mahasiddha whose instructions became central to later Himalayan Buddhist traditions.

Why Pashupatinath enters the story

Pashupatinath is best known as one of Hinduism's most important Shiva shrines. Yet the wider precinct has long been a meeting ground for several religious communities. Kathmandu Metropolitan City's heritage description notes the presence of Shaiva, Shakta, Vaishnava and Buddhist traditions, among others, within the wider Pashupati landscape.

That layered setting helps explain why Buddhist memories and sacred sites are encountered around the area. It does not, by itself, prove that a specific cave was used by Naropa or Tilopa. The responsible way to describe these places is as sites associated with them in local or religious tradition, unless stronger historical or archaeological evidence is available.

What can you actually visit?

Start with the wider Pashupatinath precinct rather than setting out in search of an unverified cave entrance. Walk the public approaches and riverfront areas, take time to understand the layout, and ask a local heritage guide or site caretaker whether any Buddhist shrine or cave traditionally linked with Naropa and Tilopa is currently accessible. Do not enter enclosed or secluded religious spaces without permission.

Plan a respectful visit

GETTING THERE

Pashupatinath is around 5 km east of central Kathmandu. Taxis, local buses and three-wheelers can take visitors towards Gaushala, from where the temple area is a short walk. Allow extra time for traffic.

ENTRY & ACCESS

Nepal Tourism Board states that the main temple courtyard is open to Hindus; visitors of other faiths can view the temple area from the terraces across the Bagmati. Access rules for other shrines and spaces may differ.

PHOTOGRAPHY & CONDUCT

Follow posted restrictions. Photography is prohibited in the main temple complex, and visitors should be especially careful around worshippers, funerals and cremation areas. Ask before photographing people.

TIME TO ALLOW

Set aside at least a couple of hours for the public riverfront and surrounding heritage area. A guide can add context and help identify which spaces are open on the day.

A NOTE ON THE CAVES

We have not found a reliable public source that establishes the exact cave locations, archaeological dates or visitor access for sites locally associated with Naropa and Tilopa near Pashupatinath. We will not invent a pin, route or opening time. Ask a recognised local guide or site caretaker before making a special journey for a specific cave.

Why the story is still worth following

The Naropa–Tilopa story is not just a search for a hidden cave. It is a story about the relationship between learning and experience, and about how teachings travelled from medieval India into the Himalayan world. Pashupatinath offers a remarkable place to think about the many traditions that have shared Kathmandu's sacred geography—even when the exact history of an individual shrine remains uncertain.
