The Last Table
by the Window

The rain had emptied the terrace. The city had gone soft around the edges. And the only table left was the one neither of them had planned to share.

AN ORIGINAL VRIDHI STORY · FICTION · APPROX. 5 MIN READ

At 9:43, the restaurant was almost empty. Rain drew silver lines down the windows; somewhere beyond the glass, traffic moved like a slow river of headlights.

Mira had chosen the last table because it faced the street. She liked watching people hurry through weather they had not prepared for. She had not chosen the stranger who arrived carrying a damp jacket and an apologetic smile.

“Every other table is taken,” he said, looking at the empty room.

She glanced at the chairs, then back at him. “A convincing opening argument.”

He laughed. Not loudly. Just enough to make the waiter look over.

His name was Arjun. He asked before sitting. She noticed that. He noticed that she noticed, and for a moment neither of them looked away.

The small art of not rushing

They began with the rain, moved to the restaurant's terrible playlist, and somehow arrived at the question of whether a person could tell anything important about someone from the way they ordered coffee.

“You take yours without sugar,” he said.

“You watched me order?”

“I was trying to decide whether you looked like someone who would forgive a stranger for stealing half a table.”

“And?”

“Still collecting evidence.”

It was not the line. It was the pause after it. The way he let the joke settle instead of reaching for another one. The way she found herself leaning forward when she could have leaned back.

Outside, the rain grew heavier. Inside, the candles burned lower. They discovered a mutual dislike of voice notes longer than a minute, a shared weakness for old film songs, and the fact that both had come out that evening intending to be home by ten.

At 10:26, neither mentioned the time.

“I had a plan for tonight,” she said. “It did not include this.”
“Should I apologise?”
“Not yet.”

A question left hanging

When the bill arrived, Arjun reached for it, then stopped. “Would you prefer to split it?”

Mira smiled. “I would prefer you asked me that before deciding.”

He slid the bill between them. “Then I am asking.”

It was such a small thing. But the evening had been made of small things: the space he left between their chairs, the attention he gave her answers, the way he never treated a smile as a promise.

They stepped outside beneath the awning. The rain had softened to a mist. A cab pulled up at the curb, its indicator blinking amber against the wet road.

“I had a good time,” he said.

“You sound surprised.”

“I am trying not to sound too pleased with myself.”

“How is that going?”

“Badly.”

She laughed, and this time he did too.

He asked if he could see her again. No clever line. No assumption. Just a question, offered plainly enough for her to answer honestly.

Mira looked at the rain, then at him. “Ask me tomorrow. I want to remember tonight without deciding what it means.”

“Tomorrow, then.”

She got into the cab. As it pulled away, her phone lit up with a message from a number she had not saved.

For the record, I think you would have forgiven the table.

She watched the message until the traffic light turned green.

Then she typed: For the record, I haven't decided.

And smiled all the way home.

FICTION · All characters are adults. This is an imagined story about mutual attraction and consensual connection.
